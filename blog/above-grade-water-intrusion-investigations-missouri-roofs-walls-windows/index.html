Above-Grade Water Intrusion Investigations in Missouri: Roofs, Walls, and Windows

November 24, 2026

A stain on a ceiling or a wet windowsill is not, by itself, a plumbing loss. Water that has been entering through the roof, the wall cladding, a window, or the flashing around those assemblies can show up in the same room as a burst supply line. The pattern is often slower: staining that returns after each rain, soft sheathing, peeled paint at a head flashing, or a wall that is wet only on the weather side.

Insurers and owners in Missouri ask a building-envelope question when that is the file. Where did the water cross the exterior, how long has it been doing so, and what did it damage on the way in. A licensed Professional Engineer (PE) can document the path the evidence supports. The PE does not decide coverage, and a stain map is not a policy interpretation.

This article is about chronic leakage through roofing, cladding, windows, and flashing. Supply and drain failures are covered in plumbing leak and water-damage investigations. Ice dams, one winter roof mechanism, are covered in ice dam and roof-collapse damage. Below-grade and foundation moisture are a different assignment. This article does not constitute engineering advice or establish a professional engagement.

Above-Grade Means the Exterior Skin

Above grade, the building envelope is the roof, the walls, the windows and doors, and the flashings that join them. Those layers are supposed to shed water and, behind the finish, to drain or dry the water that gets past the first surface.

A plumbing investigation starts at a pipe, a fitting, a fixture, or a drain. An envelope investigation starts at the weather side: which elevation faces the storm, which detail is open to that elevation, and whether the interior wetting lines up with that detail rather than with a supply or a drain above.

  • Rain that appears during or just after wind-driven rain, and is dry in clear weather, points at the exterior
  • Wetting that continues with the water off, or that tracks a supply line, a tub, or a drain, points at plumbing until shown otherwise
  • Both can be true in one room. An upstairs bath and a failed kick-out flashing can stain the same ceiling. The opinion has to separate them

The Stain Is Not the Source

Water that passes the cladding does not stop at the first stud bay. It runs on underlayment, on the water-resistive barrier, on the back of sheathing, and along the top of a window, then shows up feet away from the opening. A ceiling stain under a valley can be the valley, a chimney cricket, or a plumbing vent whose flashing has opened.

That is why replacing the stained drywall, before anyone photographs the path, removes the record. The interior finish is the symptom. The investigation is the route from the weather to that finish.

Roofs

Steep-slope roofs in Missouri are commonly asphalt shingles over underlayment. Low-slope roofs are membranes. Both leak at the details more often than in the middle of a sound field.

  • Valleys, and the transition where a lower roof meets a wall
  • Step flashing and counterflashing at sidewalls and chimneys
  • Kick-out flashing at the bottom of a roof-wall intersection, where missing metal dumps water behind the cladding
  • Penetrations: vents, skylights, satellite mounts, and flue flashings that have opened or were never lapped into the underlayment
  • Eaves and rakes after wind has lifted tabs or peeled a membrane seam. Storm damage of that kind is a related file, covered in wind, hail, and tornado damage

Age, brittle shingles, and a second layer of roofing are conditions. They are not, by themselves, the opening that let this rain in. The opinion should name the detail that lines up with the wetting, and say when more than one detail is still possible.

Walls and Cladding

Wall cladding is a finish over a drainage plane. Brick, vinyl, fiber cement, and stucco all rely on flashings, weeps, and a water-resistive barrier behind them. Sealant at a joint is not a substitute for that plane when the joint is a moving one.

  • Wind-driven rain on the weather elevation, often the south or west face in Missouri, wetting one wall and not the opposite wall
  • Open ends of vinyl or lap siding, and J-channels that discharge into the wall instead of out
  • Brick veneer without a clear weep path, or mortar droppings that have filled the cavity at the base of the wall above a window
  • Through-wall flashings and head flashings that stop short of the face of the cladding, so water exits inside the backup

Interior paint bubbles on an exterior wall, with dry finishes on interior partitions, are a clue. They are not a completed path. The cladding and the flashing at that bay still have to be looked at.

Windows

Window leaks are often blamed on the glass unit. Many of them are the rough opening: the sill, the head, and the jambs around the frame.

  • No sill pan, or a pan that slopes back toward the interior, so water that passes the flange sits on the stool
  • Head flashing that does not lap over the window flange, or house wrap that was cut and left open above the head
  • Sealant used as the only weather joint, then cracked, so the next wind-driven rain has a slot
  • A window installed after the cladding, with trim that covers the flange and no visible flashing
  • Wetting at the sill that is actually a roof or wall leak running down and exiting at the window, because the window is the first opening in the path

A fogged insulated glass unit is a glazing condition. It is not the same finding as water staining on the drywall under the sill. The report should not treat them as one defect unless the evidence connects them.

Chronic Wetting and a Single Storm

Claim files turn on duration as much as on location. The physical evidence sometimes distinguishes a long-running leak from water that entered in one storm. It does not always.

  • Repeated staining in layers, rusted fasteners, deteriorated sheathing, and mold on the back of drywall that is dry on the day of the visit are consistent with wetting over time
  • Clean rupture of underlayment, a lifted shingle course with fresh nail pulls, or wetting that starts at a documented storm date and has no older staining behind it is consistent with a shorter event
  • Drying, painting, and a prior repair can erase both patterns. The opinion should say what was already gone

Missouri sees wind-driven rain in warm months and freeze-thaw at open joints in winter. Either can enlarge a gap that was already there. Naming the weather that fits the stain is part of the work. Deciding whether a policy responds to that weather is not.

What the Investigation Uses

The methods follow the question. They are not a tour of every instrument on a truck.

  • Exterior and interior mapping: elevation, height, and which details sit above the wet area
  • Moisture readings and, where useful, infrared images, as screening. A cool spot is not a leak until it is checked against a source
  • Selective opening, when the owner or the carrier authorizes it, at the flashing or sheathing the pattern points to
  • Controlled water at a suspected joint, recorded so the test can be distinguished from rain that fell the same day

A test that wets the whole wall does not identify a window head. The sequence should isolate one detail at a time, and the notes should say what was tested and what was not.

What to Preserve

Repairs that stop the leak are appropriate when the building is being damaged further. The record of the path should come first if the cause is in dispute.

  • Wide photos of the elevation and close photos of the suspect flashing, before tarps and sealant
  • The date of the photos and, if known, the rain or wind event the owner ties to the wetting
  • Interior photos of the stain edges, not only the darkest center, and any prior stain that was painted over
  • Removed flashing, a window flange, or sheathing, kept if counsel or the carrier has asked for it. Once it is in a dumpster, the lap order is gone

What the Opinion Can Cover

Depending on scope, a written review may include a site visit and a report that:

  • Describes the roof, wall, and window conditions that were observed, and the limits of access
  • Traces the interior wetting to one or more exterior details, or states that the path is not determined
  • Separates envelope leakage from plumbing, condensate, and ice-dam mechanisms when more than one is plausible
  • Comments on whether the visible damage is consistent with repeated wetting or with a shorter event, with the basis stated
  • Outlines repair at a conceptual level so an owner or a carrier can compare contractor pricing. It is not a bid, and it is not a coverage decision

How repair scope is framed after damage is discussed in repair versus replace. The general shape of a forensic assignment is in forensic engineering analysis. Services for this work are described under forensic engineering.

What to Send

  • Address, and which rooms and elevations are wet
  • When the staining was first noticed, and whether it returns with rain
  • Photos of the exterior details above the stain and of the interior stain, including any pictures from before repairs
  • Roof age, cladding type, and any recent window or roof work
  • Plumbing or HVAC notes if a carrier has already been told the loss is a pipe or a condensate line
  • The claim number and any engineer or contractor report already in the file

What This Review Does Not Replace

  • A coverage determination or a reservation-of-rights analysis
  • A plumbing opinion when the release is a supply, a drain, or an appliance connection
  • A medical or industrial-hygiene opinion about mold exposure
  • A foundation or below-grade waterproofing investigation
  • A contractor's bid. Conceptual repair scope is not a price

Closing

Above-grade water intrusion in Missouri is a building-envelope question: roofing, cladding, windows, and flashing, and whether the wetting fits repeated leakage or a shorter event. The interior stain is where the water became visible. It is not automatically where the water came in.

Photograph the exterior details and the stain edges before the opening is closed. The plumbing-leak article remains the right starting point when the source is a pipe. This one is the file when the weather side of the building is the question.
