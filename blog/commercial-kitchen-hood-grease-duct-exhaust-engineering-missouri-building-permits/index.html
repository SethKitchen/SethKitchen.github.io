Commercial Kitchen Hood, Grease Duct, and Exhaust Engineering for Missouri Building Permits

August 14, 2026

Commercial kitchens in Missouri — restaurants, school cafeterias, institutional food service, food trucks converting to brick-and-mortar, and tenant improvements converting retail to cooking uses — often trigger mechanical plan review for hoods, grease ducts, and exhaust systems. These systems remove heat, smoke, grease-laden vapor, and odors, and they interact with fire protection, makeup air, and building envelope openings.

Local jurisdictions commonly ask for drawings and calculations that show hood type, exhaust rates, duct routing and clearances, fan selection, makeup air strategy, and coordination with suppression systems. A licensed Professional Engineer (PE) may be involved when the design is non-prescriptive, when sealed mechanical plans are required, or when plan review comments need a technical response.

This article provides general information about commercial kitchen hood, grease duct, and exhaust engineering for Missouri building permits. Requirements vary by city and county, adopted codes, and project scope.

Why Kitchen Exhaust Systems Draw Plan Review Attention

Kitchen exhaust is not treated like ordinary HVAC ductwork. Grease deposits, high temperatures, fire risk, and the need for cleanable, continuously welded or listed grease duct assemblies make these systems a frequent focus of mechanical and fire plan review.

Reviewers typically look for documentation that addresses:

  • Whether the cooking equipment requires a Type I or Type II hood
  • Exhaust airflow rates based on equipment, hood style, and applicable code or manufacturer criteria
  • Grease duct material, slope, cleanouts, clearances, and listed assemblies where used
  • Fan location, access, and discharge away from openings and property lines as required
  • Makeup air quantity, temperature, and how it is introduced so the hood can capture properly
  • Fire suppression coverage for the hood and cooking appliances

Incomplete submittals often lead to comments on duct clearances, makeup air imbalance, missing equipment schedules, or insufficient detail on grease duct construction.

Type I vs. Type II Hoods

Distinguishing hood type is a basic permit question:

Type I hoods

Type I hoods are generally used for grease-producing or smoke-producing cooking equipment such as fryers, griddles, ranges, broilers, and similar appliances. They typically require grease filters or extractors, grease duct systems, and coordination with a fire suppression system.

Type II hoods

Type II hoods are generally used for heat, steam, or odor removal where grease-laden vapor is not expected — for example, some dishwashers, ovens, or steam equipment, depending on the equipment and local interpretation. They do not usually involve grease duct construction, but exhaust rates, duct routing, and makeup air still matter.

Misclassifying equipment as Type II when grease is produced is a common cause of plan rejection or field correction. Equipment schedules and manufacturer data should support the hood selection shown on the drawings.

Grease Ducts and Exhaust Routing

Grease ducts convey grease-laden exhaust from the hood to the outdoors. Engineering and permit documents often need to show:

  • Continuous, cleanable duct path with slope toward collection points or as required by the adopted code and listing
  • Access panels or cleanouts at changes in direction and other required locations
  • Clearances to combustibles, or use of listed grease duct wrap/enclosure systems where clearances are reduced
  • Penetration details through walls, floors, and roofs, including fire-resistance-rated assemblies when applicable
  • Fan type and mounting (for example, rooftop upblast fans common on commercial kitchens)
  • Discharge location relative to air intakes, operable windows, property lines, and neighboring uses

Tenant spaces in older Missouri buildings can be especially challenging when the only practical duct path crosses rated construction, limited ceiling cavities, or shared shafts. Those conditions often need engineered detailing rather than a catalog sketch.

Makeup Air and Kitchen Balance

Exhaust without adequate makeup air can create negative pressure, door draft problems, poor hood capture, and comfort complaints. Missouri plan reviewers frequently ask how makeup air will be provided and tempered.

Typical documentation may include:

  • Exhaust CFM and makeup air CFM for each hood or system
  • Whether makeup air is dedicated, transferred, or a combination
  • Heating or cooling of makeup air when required by code or project conditions
  • Interlocks so fans and makeup air operate in the intended sequence
  • Effects on adjacent dining, retail, or residential spaces in mixed-use buildings

Change-of-use projects that add cooking to a space previously designed for retail or office occupancy often discover that existing HVAC capacity and outdoor air openings are not enough without a dedicated kitchen exhaust and makeup air design.

Fire Protection and Life-Safety Coordination

Type I hood systems are commonly paired with automatic fire suppression protecting the hood, filters, plenums, and cooking appliances. Permit packages may need both mechanical and fire protection information, even when separate contractors design each portion.

Coordination items that affect engineering review include:

  • Appliance layout under the hood and nozzle coverage assumptions
  • Fuel or power shutoff interlocks upon system activation
  • Exhaust fan operation during or after a suppression event as required by the applicable standard and manufacturer instructions
  • Clearances and access for inspection and servicing of both duct and suppression components

A mechanical drawing set that ignores suppression or fuel shutoff is a frequent source of comments. Cross-referencing the suppression submittal helps plan review and field inspection.

When Missouri Building Permits May Require Engineered Documents

Whether sealed PE drawings are required depends on the jurisdiction, occupancy, and how the local building department applies its adopted mechanical and building codes. Engineered documentation is more often requested when:

  • A new commercial kitchen or major hood replacement is proposed
  • Grease ducts penetrate rated construction or travel long distances through a building
  • Makeup air and exhaust affect multiple zones or a shared HVAC system
  • The project is a change of occupancy or use that adds cooking
  • Plan review comments ask for calculations, listed assembly details, or a design professional response
  • The AHJ requires mechanical plans prepared by a licensed design professional for commercial work

Even when a hood manufacturer provides catalog literature, jurisdictions may still want site-specific drawings showing routing, clearances, fan schedules, and makeup air. Catalog cut sheets alone rarely replace a coordinated mechanical plan set.

What a Typical Permit Submittal May Include

A Missouri commercial kitchen mechanical package often includes some combination of:

  • Floor plan showing cooking equipment, hood footprint, and clearances
  • Hood schedule (type, size, listed exhaust rate or calculated rate, filters)
  • Exhaust duct plan and riser or section showing size, material, slope, cleanouts, and roof penetration
  • Fan schedule and electrical characteristics for coordination
  • Makeup air plan, diffuser locations, and heating/cooling notes
  • Details for grease duct enclosure, wrap, or clearance reductions using listed systems
  • Notes referencing applicable IMC/IFC/NFPA provisions as adopted locally, plus manufacturer requirements

Separate architectural, electrical, plumbing, and fire protection sheets may still be required. Kitchen projects often fail plan review because one discipline assumes another will cover a detail that nobody submitted.

Common Plan Review Comments

  • Hood type not justified by the cooking equipment schedule
  • Exhaust CFM shown without basis or inconsistent with makeup air
  • Missing grease duct cleanouts, slope, or clearance details
  • Fan discharge too close to openings, intakes, or property lines
  • No interlock or sequence description for exhaust and makeup air
  • Suppression coverage or fuel shutoff not coordinated with the mechanical layout
  • Roof curb, structural support, or penetration details omitted for rooftop fans

How a Professional Engineer May Help

A Missouri PE supporting kitchen hood and exhaust permitting may:

  • Prepare or review mechanical drawings and calculations for hood exhaust and makeup air
  • Detail grease duct routing, clearances, and listed enclosure systems for constrained buildings
  • Coordinate with architects, kitchen designers, and fire protection contractors so submittals align
  • Respond to plan review comments and resubmittals with engineering documentation
  • Evaluate existing systems when a remodel adds equipment or changes the menu profile

Engineers do not replace the AHJ's authority to interpret and enforce local codes. Scope, sealing, and responsible charge depend on the assignment and what the jurisdiction requires.

Practical Guidance for Owners and Contractors

  • Confirm early whether the jurisdiction treats the work as a mechanical permit, alteration, or change of use
  • Finalize the equipment list before locking hood size and exhaust rates
  • Plan grease duct path and fan location before lease or architectural commitments make routing impractical
  • Budget for makeup air, not only the hood and fan
  • Coordinate suppression, gas/electric shutoffs, and mechanical drawings in one submittal package when possible
  • Expect field inspection of duct construction, clearances, and fan installation in addition to paper plan review

Closing

Commercial kitchen hood, grease duct, and exhaust systems are a common Missouri permitting pinch point because they combine mechanical performance, fire risk, and building-envelope penetrations. Clear drawings that define hood type, exhaust and makeup air, grease duct construction, and fire-protection coordination help move plan review forward and reduce field surprises.
