Fire Alarm Systems in Missouri: When Should a Professional Engineer Be Involved?

September 21, 2026

Fire alarm systems detect smoke, heat, or manual pull stations and notify occupants and, in many buildings, the fire department. They are a different system from sprinklers and other suppression equipment, even though the two are often coordinated and reviewed together.

In Missouri, licensed fire-alarm contractors design and install many conventional and addressable systems under the adopted fire and building codes. A licensed Professional Engineer (PE) becomes more likely when the authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) wants sealed drawings, when voice-evacuation or high-rise control is involved, or when an existing building, change of occupancy, or sprinkler interface does not fit a catalog layout.

This article provides general information about fire alarm systems in Missouri and when PE involvement may be appropriate. It does not constitute engineering or fire-code advice or establish a professional engagement. Confirm current requirements with the local building and fire departments.

What a Fire Alarm System Includes

A fire alarm installation is more than a panel and a few smoke detectors. Typical scope includes:

  • Initiating devices: smoke and heat detectors, duct detectors, pull stations, and waterflow or tamper switches from a sprinkler system
  • Notification appliances: horns, strobes, speakers, and visible-notification coverage
  • The fire alarm control unit, power supplies, and battery calculations
  • Monitoring or supervising-station connection where required
  • Optional emergency voice/alarm communication (voice-evac) and firefighter communication features
  • Sequences that shut down HVAC, release doors, or recall elevators when the adopted code requires those interfaces

Suppression systems control or extinguish fire with water or other agents. Alarm systems detect and notify. Both may be required in the same building; a sprinkler permit set does not automatically satisfy fire-alarm review.

Codes, Occupancy, and Local Review

Missouri cities and counties adopt building and fire codes that reference fire-alarm standards (commonly NFPA 72) and occupancy rules in the International Building Code / International Fire Code editions they have adopted. Whether a fire alarm is required — and how elaborate it must be — depends on occupancy, occupant load, stories, and whether the building is sprinklered.

Plan review may sit with the building department, the fire marshal, or both. Schools, churches, assembly spaces, high-rises, and health-care occupancies are more likely to draw detailed alarm review than a small office with a few detectors.

Existing-building work, tenant improvements, and changes of occupancy often trigger alarm upgrades even when the original building never had a full system. Those projects overlap life-safety evaluations and TCO closeout more than a new-warehouse sprinkler layout.

When a Professional Engineer May Be Involved

Many single-story commercial replacements and small additions proceed with contractor shop drawings and a licensed fire-alarm company. PE involvement is more likely when:

  • The AHJ requires sealed fire-alarm drawings or a PE letter above a stated occupancy, height, or system type
  • Voice-evacuation, high-rise, or mass-notification features are part of the design
  • The system must coordinate with sprinklers, kitchen suppression, smoke control, or elevator recall
  • An existing building is being evaluated for occupancy, assembly use, or code-compliance gaps
  • Notification coverage, occupant load, or audibility in large or irregular spaces is in dispute
  • Plan-review comments ask for sequences, battery calculations, or interface diagrams the installer package does not include
  • Alternative methods or delayed-egress / special locking arrangements need documented life-safety justification

Engineering services may include review of contractor drawings, riser and device-layout comments, sequence-of-operation notes, permit-supporting letters, and coordination with mechanical or electrical work. Documents are sealed when appropriate and when scope supports responsible charge.

Coordination with Sprinklers and Other Systems

Reviewers often fail a fire-alarm submittal because interfaces are missing, not because detector spacing is wrong. Common coordination items include:

  • Waterflow and valve-tamper supervision from the sprinkler system
  • Kitchen hood suppression and gas or electric shutoff signals
  • HVAC shutdown or smoke-control sequences on alarm
  • Door release, stair pressurization, and elevator recall

Those interfaces belong on the fire-alarm drawings and sequence-of-operation, even when another trade designs the sprinkler, hood, or HVAC system. A PE review is often scoped to that coordination, not to redesign the entire detection layout.

What AHJs Typically Want on the Permit Set

Fire-alarm plan review commonly looks for:

  • Floor plans with device locations and notification coverage
  • Riser diagram and panel / power-supply information
  • Battery calculations and voltage-drop notes where required
  • Sequence of operation, including sprinkler and HVAC interfaces
  • Cut sheets for listed equipment and compatibility with the control unit
  • Monitoring arrangement and firefighter-response notes the local fire department expects

Shop drawings prepared after the building permit is issued may still need AHJ or PE review before installation. Deferred fire-alarm packages are a frequent source of inspection delay when they are left off the first permit set.

Practical Guidance for Owners and Contractors

  • Ask early whether fire review is with the building department, the fire marshal, or both
  • Do not assume a sprinkler set covers fire-alarm permitting
  • Show sprinkler, hood, and HVAC interfaces on the alarm sequence, not only in a brochure
  • For existing buildings, confirm whether occupancy or occupant load is changing before locking a "like-for-like" replacement
  • Engage a PE when the AHJ asks for sealed drawings, voice-evac, or coordination the installer package cannot document

Closing

Fire alarm systems in Missouri are life-safety electrical and fire-code work, not a subset of sprinkler design. Licensed contractors handle many standard installations. A Professional Engineer may help when sealed drawings are required, when voice-evacuation or multi-system interfaces are involved, or when an existing building's occupancy and notification coverage need an independent technical basis.
