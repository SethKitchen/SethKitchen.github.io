Carbon Monoxide and HVAC Failure Investigations in Missouri: When a PE Report Helps

September 23, 2026

Carbon monoxide (CO) claims and HVAC failures often arrive at an insurer as two facts: occupants reported illness or a CO alarm, and a furnace, boiler, water heater, or vent was later found defective. Between those facts is the engineering question. Did combustion equipment actually produce and deliver CO into occupied space, and through what path?

Missouri winters make furnace and venting losses common. A cracked heat exchanger, blocked flue, disconnected vent, failed inducer, or missing combustion air can be alleged in the same file as a service invoice that simply says "replaced furnace." Once the unit is removed, the mechanism is harder to reconstruct. A licensed Professional Engineer (PE) may inspect the equipment and vent path, preserve components, and write a forensic report that states what the physical evidence supports.

This article is about investigations after a CO event or HVAC failure, not about sizing a new system for a building permit. Design and permit questions are covered separately. A PE report does not diagnose medical injury and does not decide insurance coverage. It documents equipment condition, venting, and combustion-air conditions within a defined scope.

Why These Scenes Change Quickly

Safety comes first. Fire departments and HVAC contractors are right to shut equipment down, ventilate the building, and restore heat. That work also removes the evidence an insurer may later need:

  • The appliance is swapped before anyone photographs the heat exchanger, flue, or control board
  • Vents are reconnected or replaced, erasing a separation, blockage, or improper slope
  • CO alarm history, detector location, and peak readings are not recorded
  • Combustion-air openings are unblocked or altered during the repair

A forensic HVAC investigation tries to separate as-found conditions from later repairs, and to say clearly when the original condition can no longer be evaluated.

Typical Assignment Questions from Insurers

Claims staff commonly ask a PE to address questions such as:

  • Which appliance was a plausible CO source, and which were not?
  • Was the failure consistent with a cracked heat exchanger, blocked or separated vent, inducer or pressure-switch problem, or inadequate combustion air?
  • Could products of combustion have entered occupied space by the path alleged?
  • Do installation, modification, or maintenance conditions appear relevant?
  • Was the condition more consistent with a recent failure or with longer-term deterioration?
  • What should be retained before the furnace or vent is discarded?

A request to "confirm the claim is covered" or to "state that CO caused the injury" asks for a coverage or medical conclusion. A request to describe equipment condition and a plausible transport path stays within engineering scope.

What a Missouri Forensic HVAC Investigation Often Includes

Scope depends on whether the appliance is still in place and whether other parties may have an interest in the evidence. A PE investigation may include:

  • Review of the claim file, CO alarm reports, fire-department notes, and HVAC invoices
  • Site inspection of the appliance, flue or vent connector, chimney or sidewall termination, and combustion-air openings
  • Documentation of as-found connections, corrosion, soot, spillage staining, and control or inducer condition
  • Identification of other fuel-burning appliances that could explain the same alarm
  • Retention of the heat exchanger, vent sections, or controls when destructive examination may be needed
  • A written report of observations, opinions, assumptions, and limitations

Operating a suspect furnace solely to "see if it makes CO" after the scene has been altered can be misleading and may be unsafe. Any functional test should be planned, not improvised after parts have already been replaced.

Common Furnace, Venting, and Combustion-Air Themes

Residential and light-commercial assignments in Missouri frequently involve:

  • Heat-exchanger cracks, corrosion, or separations alleged after a CO alarm
  • Flue or vent connectors that are disconnected, back-pitched, crushed, or blocked by debris, nests, or a failed cap
  • Shared chimneys, orphaned water heaters, and vent systems that no longer match the appliances connected to them
  • Inducer fans, pressure switches, and condensate drains on high-efficiency furnaces that fail to prove draft
  • Combustion-air openings that were covered, undersized, or competing with exhaust fans and clothes dryers
  • Recent service, filter neglect, or a closed-off mechanical room that changed how the appliance was intended to operate

A failed HVAC system is not automatically a CO source. A no-heat call, a cracked cabinet, or a dirty filter can be a comfort or reliability problem without a pathway for combustion products into occupied rooms. The report should distinguish those cases.

What a PE Report Can and Cannot Support

A useful forensic report for an insurer typically can:

  • Identify the appliance and vent conditions observed, including what was already repaired
  • Evaluate whether those conditions are consistent with CO production or spillage into the building
  • Note competing sources, such as other fuel-burning equipment, vehicles in an attached garage, or portable heaters
  • Describe installation or maintenance conditions that appear relevant, and those that do not
  • State limitations when the heat exchanger or vent was discarded

The same report should not assign medical causation, interpret blood tests, or apply policy language. Those questions belong to physicians, claims professionals, and counsel. Engineering causation — how the equipment behaved — is not the same as legal cause or coverage.

Evidence to Preserve

If a technical opinion may matter, insurers and contractors should consider holding, before disposal:

  • Photographs of the appliance in place, including the vent connection, flue, and mechanical-room openings
  • The heat exchanger or vent sections if a crack, hole, or blockage is alleged
  • CO alarm make, model, location, and any logged readings
  • Recent service records and the date heat was lost or restored
  • Notice to other interested parties before destructive examination of a retained furnace

Restoring heat for the occupants and preserving the failed component are compatible if the contractor is told to bag and label the old unit instead of scrapping it at the curb.

Common Pitfalls

  • Treating an HVAC invoice that says "bad heat exchanger" as a completed causation analysis
  • Inspecting only after the furnace and vent have been discarded
  • Assuming every CO alarm is a furnace failure without checking other fuel-burning sources
  • Equating soot or corrosion with a proven crack
  • Offering medical opinions about symptoms or exposure
  • Using a design-review letter about a new HVAC permit as if it were a forensic investigation of a loss

Practical Guidance for Insurers

  • Engage forensic support early when the appliance may be replaced before the next site visit
  • Ask the contractor to retain the furnace, vent connector, and relevant controls
  • Provide alarm records, fire-department notes, and service history at intake
  • Specify whether the need is a claim screen, a mechanism opinion, or support for a later recovery review
  • Keep medical and coverage questions out of the engineering assignment

Closing

Carbon monoxide and HVAC failure investigations in Missouri depend on the appliance and vent path still being available to examine. A Professional Engineer report can help an insurer understand whether furnace, venting, or combustion-air conditions are consistent with CO entering occupied space, and what cannot be concluded after repairs. It does not replace medical evaluation or a coverage decision.
