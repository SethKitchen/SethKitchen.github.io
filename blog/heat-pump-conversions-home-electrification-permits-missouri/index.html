Heat Pump Conversions and Home Electrification Permits in Missouri

October 14, 2026

Missouri homeowners are replacing gas furnaces and central air with heat pumps, adding heat-pump water heaters, and swapping ranges for induction. Contractors call that a conversion or a home electrification project. The mechanical equipment is only half of the permit path. The other half is whether the existing electrical service and panel can carry the new loads — compressor, air handler, electric resistance backup, and anything else added in the same project.

Licensed HVAC contractors and electricians complete most of this work. A licensed Professional Engineer (PE) becomes useful when the authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) wants documented sizing or a load calculation, when ductwork or a new outdoor unit does not match the existing system, or when the panel is already full and a service upgrade is part of the same permit. This note covers that overlap. Broader HVAC involvement, residential service upgrades, and solar-plus-storage have their own articles and are linked rather than repeated.

This article provides general information about heat pump conversions and home electrification permits in Missouri. It does not constitute legal or engineering advice or establish a professional engagement. Rules differ by city and county. Confirm current mechanical and electrical permit requirements with the local building department and the serving utility before ordering equipment.

What a Heat Pump Conversion Usually Includes

A typical conversion replaces a gas furnace and outdoor condenser with a heat pump and air handler, or adds a heat pump while keeping a furnace as backup (dual fuel). Scope often includes:

  • Outdoor unit and indoor coil or air handler
  • Refrigerant line set and condensate drainage
  • Duct connections, or limited duct changes at the air handler
  • Electrical circuit for the outdoor unit, indoor unit, and any electric heat strips
  • Thermostat and controls, including changeover for dual fuel
  • Sometimes a new disconnect, subpanel, or service upgrade if the existing panel cannot accept the load

Home electrification is broader than one heat pump. It can also mean a heat-pump water heater, induction range, EV charger, or electric dryer added in the same year. Each item is a load. The permit question is whether they are submitted together or as separate mechanical and electrical permits.

Mechanical Side: Sizing and Installation Limits

Heat-pump capacity is not the same number as the old furnace's input rating. Contractors size equipment from the dwelling's heating and cooling loads, climate, and duct system — not from a rule of thumb that matches tonnage to square footage alone. An oversized unit short-cycles. An undersized unit leans on electric resistance heat, which is exactly the load that fills a panel.

Permit reviewers in Missouri cities and counties may ask for equipment data, a load summary, or a simple layout when the work is more than a like-for-like condenser swap. Common mechanical flags include:

  • New outdoor unit location, pad, or setback from property lines
  • Duct modifications, added returns, or a relocated air handler
  • Dual-fuel controls and when the furnace is allowed to run
  • Condensate routing that ties into plumbing, especially in a finished basement or attic
  • Combustion-air or venting changes if a gas furnace or water heater remains

A PE is more likely on the mechanical side when the AHJ wants sealed load documentation, when an addition is happening at the same time, or when existing ducts cannot support the proposed airflow. Day-to-day furnace and heat-pump replacement practice is covered in HVAC systems and when a PE may be involved.

Electrical Side: Panel Capacity and New Circuits

The outdoor heat pump and the air handler each need a circuit. Electric heat strips — used for backup or for cold-weather capacity — can be the largest new load in the house, often larger than the compressor. A heat-pump water heater or induction range added in the same project stacks on top of that.

Many Missouri dwellings still have 100 A or 150 A services, or a 200 A panel that is already full of tandem breakers. Electrification stalls when:

  • There is no spare breaker space for the new circuits
  • A load calculation shows the service cannot carry the heat strips plus existing ranges, dryers, and HVAC
  • The meter, mast, or main disconnect must change with the service
  • The utility requires a service application before the mechanical permit can close

How residential load calculations and 100 A to 200 A upgrades are documented is in residential electrical load calculations and service upgrades. This article only covers why a heat pump conversion is a common reason those calculations get requested.

Permit Triggers on a Missouri Heat Pump Project

Cities and counties do not use one statewide heat-pump permit form. Owners and contractors should expect some combination of the following, depending on the AHJ:

Mechanical permit

Replacement of a furnace, air conditioner, or heat pump, new refrigerant equipment, or duct changes. Some jurisdictions treat a straight equipment swap as a mechanical permit with manufacturer data; others want a load summary when capacity changes.

Electrical permit

New circuits, a subpanel, heat-strip feeders, or a service change. If the HVAC contractor's electrician pulls this permit separately, the mechanical and electrical reviews still need to describe the same equipment.

Utility service application

Required when the meter or service size changes. That step sits beside the building permit and can set the schedule for energizing the new equipment.

Plumbing only if the scope includes it

Condensate tied to a drain, or a heat-pump water heater that replaces a gas unit and changes venting or gas piping, can pull plumbing into the same project. Gas appliance removal should be shown so abandoned vents and fuel lines are not left undocumented.

When a Professional Engineer Is Usually Involved

Most single-family heat pump replacements do not start with a PE. Involvement is more common when one of these is true:

  • The AHJ asks for sealed heating and cooling load documentation or a one-line with the new HVAC loads
  • Electric heat strips plus other new appliances push the dwelling toward a service upgrade
  • The project includes an addition, a finished attic, or a duct system that is being rebuilt rather than reconnected
  • Dual fuel, a generator interlock, or a new subpanel makes the electrical scope more than a pair of dedicated circuits
  • A lender, insurer, or rebate program wants an engineer's letter that the proposed equipment and service are compatible

Mechanical and electrical scopes can be the same PE or two disciplines, depending on the question. Sealing applies when the scope supports it and the AHJ or client requires sealed documents. MEP engineering review and residential electrical describe related services on this site.

Adjacent Projects That Should Stay Separate

Heat pumps show up next to other electrification work. Those paths have their own permit triggers:

  • Solar PV and battery storage — interconnection, backfeed, and storage, not heat-pump sizing
  • EV charger and service upgrade permits — a dedicated charger load that often shares the same panel problem as heat strips
  • Standby generators and transfer switches — backup power coordination, including whether the heat pump is on the generator
  • Engineered mechanical plans for building permits — when a full mechanical drawing set is the question, not a single equipment change-out

If solar, an EV charger, and a heat pump are planned in one year, the useful engineering step is one load picture of the service, not three unrelated permit narratives that each assume spare capacity.

What to Send When Asking About PE Involvement

  • City or county and whether a permit has already been denied or commented
  • Existing service size, panel photo, and main breaker rating
  • Proposed heat pump model, heat-strip kilowatts, and air-handler data
  • Whether a gas furnace stays as dual fuel
  • Other new loads: water heater, range, EV charger, solar
  • Sketch or photos of the air handler, ducts, and outdoor unit location
  • Utility name if a service change is already in discussion
