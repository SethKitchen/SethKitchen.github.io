Garage Conversions and ADUs in Missouri: Egress, Framing, and MEP Permit Triggers

November 2, 2026

Turning an existing garage into living space is a different permit from building a new room on the back of the house. The shell is already there. The questions are the header over the old vehicle door, whether a bedroom has a real emergency escape, whether the ceiling is still tall enough after finishes, and whether the electrical service and the heating system can take a room that used to be a garage.

An accessory dwelling unit, or ADU, is the stricter version of the same project: the garage becomes its own dwelling, with a place to sleep, cook, and bathe, not a den that opens into the existing house. Missouri cities treat those two scopes differently. A building permit for a bedroom is not zoning approval for a second unit.

This article provides general information about garage conversion permits and when an ADU engineer's documentation comes up. It does not constitute engineering advice or establish a professional engagement. Cities and counties adopt and enforce codes locally.

A Converted Garage and an ADU Are Not the Same Permit

Owners use both names for drywall in the garage. Plan review sorts them by use.

  • Garage conversion. The bay becomes habitable space inside the existing house: a bedroom, a family room, an office, sometimes a bath. There is still one dwelling. The door from the old garage into the kitchen may stay as an ordinary interior door.
  • ADU. A second dwelling in the same building or in a detached garage. It has sleeping, a bathroom, and some form of cooking, and it is occupied separately from the main house. Zoning decides whether that unit is allowed at all.

Missouri does not run one statewide ADU program. Some cities have an accessory-dwelling ordinance. Others have no ADU category, and a range or a second kitchen is read as a second dwelling the zoning district may not allow. A project can be structurally sound and still be denied because the lot, the parking count, or the district does not allow another unit. Confirm the zoning reading before the header is designed around a kitchen that the city will not accept.

Losing the parking bay is its own comment. Many ordinances count off-street spaces, and the garage was one of them. Replacing that space in the driveway, or showing that the district does not require it, is a zoning sheet, not a beam calculation.

The Header at the Old Door Opening

The wall over a garage door is carrying the wall above it and, depending on the roof or the story over the garage, the framing that bears there. The header was sized for that opening. What happens to it is the structural half of a garage conversion permit.

  • Infill under the header. The vehicle door comes out, a stud wall and windows go in below the existing header, and the span does not get longer. Reviewers still want the header shown, because the new wall is not what is holding the house up. The header is.
  • A new opening. Widening the old door, removing the dropped header for a flush ceiling, or cutting a patio door in a different bay is a new beam. That is the same calculation as removing a load-bearing wall: member size, posts, and where the reactions land.
  • A story over the garage. Bedroom joists or a bonus room already sit on that header. Changing it is not a one-story gable problem. The floor above has to be in the load path.

A prescriptive header table in the adopted residential code covers some simple openings and stops at the limits printed on the table. Garage doors are often wider than those limits, and an existing built-up header is not automatically adequate because it has been there. If the city asks for sealed calculations, the work product is the header or beam, the posts, and a sketch of the opening. Scope for that kind of review is described in small residential structural reviews.

Egress When the Garage Door Is Gone

A garage is not a sleeping room, so it was never required to have a bedroom escape window. Once a bedroom is placed in that bay, the adopted residential code treats it as a sleeping room. The vehicle door does not survive as the escape path. It is being removed.

Sleeping rooms commonly need an emergency escape and rescue opening to the outdoors: a window or a door with a clear opening the adopted code accepts, a sill low enough to climb out, and a path that does not depend on going back through the house. Exact width, height, and area are in the edition the city has adopted. A single pane that looks large on a drawing and does not meet the clear-opening size is a standard correction.

  • A bedroom whose only exit is the old service door into the kitchen fails that test unless a qualifying opening is added in the exterior wall.
  • An ADU usually needs its own exit to the yard or the street, not only a door into the main house. The unit has to be leaveable if the house side is closed.
  • A window well, a security grille, or a sill set high under the header can undo an opening that would otherwise qualify.

Egress is a code-plan item the drawings have to show. It becomes an engineering item when the escape window does not fit under the existing header and the opening has to be recut.

Ceiling Height After the Garage Is Finished

Habitable rooms in the residential code are commonly required to be at least 7 feet clear, with a lower allowance for bathrooms and with limited exceptions under beams and sloped ceilings. The adopted edition controls the number and the exceptions. Garages were not designed to that rule.

The height that matters is the finished ceiling, not the bottom of the joists on the day the door is measured.

  • Drywall, furring, and insulation under the joists can consume the last inch of a garage that was barely 7 feet to the framing
  • A dropped garage-door header, a duct, or a beam across the bay sets a lower clear height in the only path through the room
  • Raising a floor to take out the step at the old house door, or to cover a slab that slopes toward the vehicle door, lowers the ceiling by the same amount

If the clear height only works by removing the dropped header, the structural scope and the ceiling-height scope are the same beam. Measure to the underside of the joists, to the underside of any beam or duct that will stay, and state the floor build-up. A rendering with a tall ceiling is not that measurement.

What Stays a Garage, and What Does Not

An attached garage is separated from the house for a reason: vehicles, fumes, and a different fire exposure. The residential code's garage-separation rules (gypsum, a closer on the door, limits on openings into sleeping rooms) apply to the wall between the garage and the dwelling.

Convert the whole bay and that wall is no longer a garage wall. It becomes an ordinary interior wall, and the separation requirement moves or goes away with the cars. Leave one bay as a garage and the separation has to be rebuilt on the new line, including the door, the penetrations, and any duct that would pull garage air into a bedroom.

A water heater or a furnace that was allowed in the garage is often not allowed in a sleeping room or in the new habitable space without separation and combustion air. Moving that appliance is a mechanical permit item that shows up on the same drawings as the bedroom. It is a frequent reason a "drywall only" conversion is sent back.

Electrical Load

The garage circuit was lighting and a few receptacles. Living space adds bedroom and general-lighting load and, if the scope grows, a bath, a kitchenette, and heating and cooling equipment. An ADU adds a dwelling load: cooking, laundry, and often its own HVAC, sometimes on a subpanel or a second meter.

Whether the existing service can carry that is a load calculation, not a guess from the number of empty breaker slots. How the calculation is built, and when a Professional Engineer is involved, is covered in residential electrical load calculations and service upgrades. A heat pump in the new room ties the electrical permit to the mechanical one; see heat pump conversions.

The new rooms also pick up the receptacle, lighting, and AFCI/GFCI rules for the use on the drawing. A garage receptacle circuit left in place does not satisfy a bedroom or a kitchenette.

HVAC and Plumbing Triggers

Habitable rooms need heat. A garage is usually outside the house's conditioned volume, or it is only tempered. The permit has to say which system will heat and cool the new space.

  • Extending the existing furnace and air conditioner, which depends on whether that equipment and the ducts have capacity left
  • A separate heat pump or ductless unit for the conversion or the ADU, with its own electrical load
  • Insulation of walls and a ceiling that were never a residential envelope, including the old door opening

When the jurisdiction asks for sealed mechanical drawings, that submittal is described in engineered mechanical plans. A new bath, a kitchenette, or an ADU kitchen raises the same question for plumbing supply, drains, and vents. See engineered plumbing plans. A stove is also the fixture that can reclassify the project from living space to a second dwelling. Put it on the plan if it is in the project, and keep it off the plan if it is not.

Attached Bay, Detached Garage, Already Finished

An attached garage converted to a room in the house is an alteration of the dwelling. A detached garage converted to an ADU is closer to a change of use of an accessory building: its own exit, its own heat, and often its own electrical feeder. How a change of use triggers engineering documentation is covered in change of occupancy. A new footprint on the back of the house is a different project, covered in planning a home addition.

Garages that were finished years ago without a permit still need the same header, egress, ceiling, and MEP story if the city is asked to recognize the space. That path is covered in engineering documentation for unpermitted work. Photos of the header and the ceiling framing are harder to get after the drywall is up, and the review has to say what was assumed.

What to Send for a Review

The useful package answers use first, then framing, then equipment.

  • Address and the city or county that issues the permit
  • Attached or detached, and whether any bay stays a garage
  • One dwelling or a separate unit with its own kitchen, bath, and entrance
  • Photos of the garage-door header, both sides if the trim can come off, and of the ceiling framing or the joists above
  • Clear height today to the joists and to any beam or duct, plus the floor build-up you plan
  • A sketch of the new rooms, the escape window or door, and the stair or step at the house
  • Panel photo, main breaker rating, and the new equipment: heat pump, range, dryer, well, or a subpanel for an ADU
  • Where the water heater and furnace are now, if they sit in the garage
  • Any plan-review comments already in hand

What This Engineering Does Not Replace

A garage-conversion review does not:

  • Guarantee that a jurisdiction will approve the permit
  • Grant zoning approval for an ADU, a second kitchen, or the loss of a required parking space
  • Design a new slab or a foundation. Leveling, slope, and the step at the house door are finish and height questions in this article. Structural slab work is a different scope.
  • Replace an architectural floor plan, or decide that a wet bar is not a kitchen
  • Size a header from a door width with no information about what bears on the wall

It does document the header at the old opening when that opening changes, and it flags the egress, ceiling, electrical, and HVAC items that turn a garage into living space. Framing scope sits with residential structural review. Service size sits with residential electrical. Local permit context is in the city permit requirements note.

Closing

A garage conversion permit in Missouri is the header over the old door, an emergency escape if someone will sleep there, a ceiling that is still tall enough after finishes, and an electrical and HVAC load the house did not have when the bay stored a car. An ADU adds a second dwelling, which is a zoning decision before it is a beam size.

Show the use, the opening, and the clear height on the drawings before the vehicle door is removed. The header is easiest to document while it is still visible.
