Temporary Certificate of Occupancy (TCO) in Missouri: Engineering Conditions and Closeout Docs

July 28, 2026

Contractors and developers often reach the end of a Missouri project with most work complete — enough for tenants to move in, inventory to stock, or a closing to proceed — while punch-list items, deferred systems, or incomplete documentation still block a final Certificate of Occupancy. In many jurisdictions, a Temporary Certificate of Occupancy (TCO) is the bridge: limited occupancy under stated conditions until closeout is finished.

TCOs are not a shortcut around life-safety requirements. They are an administrative tool that lets the authority having jurisdiction authorize partial or temporary use while remaining work and engineering closeout documents are tracked to completion. Missed conditions, expired dates, or weak documentation are a common path from "almost done" back into stop-work or failed final inspection.

This article provides general information about Temporary Certificates of Occupancy in Missouri and when Professional Engineer (PE) conditions and closeout documentation may matter. It does not constitute legal or engineering advice or establish a professional engagement. Procedures and naming vary by city and county — confirm requirements with the local building department for your permit.

What a TCO Usually Means

A Temporary Certificate of Occupancy generally authorizes use of a building or portion of a building for a limited period, subject to conditions, while remaining work is completed and inspected. Related terms that get mixed together include:

  • Certificate of Occupancy (CO) — final authorization to occupy after approved plans, inspections, and required documentation are complete
  • Temporary Certificate of Occupancy (TCO) — time-limited occupancy with listed conditions and a path to final CO
  • Partial occupancy / phased occupancy — use of completed areas while other areas remain under construction (sometimes handled separately from a TCO)
  • Conditional approval — informal phrase for occupancy allowed only if stated items are closed out by a deadline

Local portals and staff may use slightly different labels. The practical question is the same: what is allowed now, what remains outstanding, and what documents close the permit.

When Missouri Jurisdictions Commonly Issue a TCO

Building departments may consider a TCO when the project is substantially complete for life safety, but discrete items remain — for example:

  • Final landscaping, paving, or site work deferred by weather or sequencing
  • Non-critical finish work or tenant improvements still underway in limited areas
  • Outstanding special-inspection reports, test-and-balance, or commissioning paperwork
  • Pending as-built drawings, engineer letters, or deferred submittal closeouts
  • Utility, fire-department, or health-department sign-offs that trail the building inspection sequence
  • Phased commercial or multi-family projects where one wing or floor is ready before another

A TCO is less likely when egress, fire protection, structural integrity, accessibility for the occupied area, or other core life-safety systems are incomplete. Occupancy pressure from a lease start date does not override those thresholds.

Common Engineering Conditions Attached to a TCO

When engineering is involved in the original permit, TCO conditions often track unfinished technical closeout rather than new design. Typical conditions include:

Special inspection and structural observation closeout

Final reports confirming inspected work matches approved drawings, including any nonconformance resolutions.

Engineer letters addressing field changes

Documentation that substitutions, RFIs, or as-built conditions remain acceptable under the sealed design intent.

System performance and commissioning records

For MEP-heavy projects: test reports, balancing data, fire-alarm / suppression certification, or emergency-power verification still outstanding at temporary occupancy.

Deferred submittals and product documentation

Shop drawings, ICC-ES / ESR confirmation, or manufacturer installation certifications promised during plan review.

Phased or partial-occupancy separations

Confirmation that occupied areas are properly separated from active construction and that means of egress remain compliant for the occupied load.

Conditions are usually written into the TCO or an attached punch list with dates. Treating them as informal reminders is a common reason final CO is delayed or revoked.

Closeout Documents Reviewers Often Expect

Exact packages vary, but contractors and developers preparing for final CO after a TCO frequently assemble:

  • Completed inspection history and cleared corrections
  • Final special-inspection, testing, and commissioning reports
  • As-built or record drawings when field conditions changed from approved plans
  • PE letters or sealed revisions for structural / MEP changes made during construction
  • Fire, life-safety, and accessibility closeout certifications required locally
  • Energy, elevation, or other specialty certificates when the permit required them
  • Affidavits or statements of completion from responsible design professionals where the jurisdiction asks for them

Incomplete or inconsistent closeout packages are a frequent reason final inspections stall even when the building looks finished in the field.

Contractor and Developer Risks After a TCO Issues

  • Occupying beyond the TCO expiration without an extension or final CO
  • Opening areas that were never included in the temporary approval
  • Changing use or occupancy classification after temporary approval without revisiting permits
  • Losing track of engineering conditions while chasing finishes and tenant turnover
  • Assuming a TCO cures earlier plan-review or stop-work issues — it does not

Lease commencement, lender draw schedules, and marketing dates should be built around realistic closeout timelines, not the optimistic date on the TCO application.

How a Professional Engineer Can Help

  • Clarify which outstanding items are engineering closeout versus contractor punch list
  • Prepare or review letters addressing field changes, deferred items, and as-built conditions
  • Coordinate special-inspection and observation documentation for final submittal
  • Support responses when the jurisdiction questions whether TCO conditions have been met
  • Help sequence phased occupancy so occupied areas remain code- compliant while unfinished work continues

Early involvement — ideally before requesting a TCO — reduces the chance that temporary occupancy is granted on conditions the project cannot meet by the stated deadline.

Practical Steps Before You Request a TCO

  1. Ask the building department whether temporary or partial occupancy is available for your permit type
  2. Inventory life-safety systems for the areas you intend to occupy
  3. List every open inspection, deferred submittal, and engineering document still outstanding
  4. Assign owners and dates to each proposed TCO condition before applying
  5. Confirm fire, utility, and other agency sign-offs that sit outside the building permit
  6. Align lease, financing, and turnover dates with a realistic path from TCO to final CO

Closing

A Temporary Certificate of Occupancy in Missouri can help contractors and developers bridge from substantial completion to final closeout — but only when life-safety thresholds are met and engineering conditions are tracked to documented completion. Treat TCO conditions and closeout packages as part of the critical path, not paperwork to clean up after move-in.
