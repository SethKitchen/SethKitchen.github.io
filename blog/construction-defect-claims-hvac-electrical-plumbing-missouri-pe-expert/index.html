Construction Defect Claims for HVAC, Electrical, and Plumbing in Missouri: When a PE Expert Helps

October 16, 2026

Counsel and insurers in Missouri see construction defect files that are not about foundations or framing. The complaint is a heat pump that never heated the house, a panel installed without required clearances, repeated leaks at a new bath, or a commercial kitchen exhaust that failed inspection. Those are mechanical, electrical, and plumbing (MEP) questions: how the system was designed or installed, how it performs, and whether the work met the standard of care that applied at the time.

A licensed Professional Engineer (PE) can help when the file needs a technical opinion a contractor's repair quote cannot carry — mechanism, departure from applicable codes or accepted practice, and what repair scope follows from the observed conditions. The PE does not decide coverage, allocate fault as a legal conclusion, or replace counsel's theory of the case.

This article provides general information about HVAC, electrical, and plumbing construction defect claims in Missouri. It does not address structural defects. It does not constitute legal or engineering advice or establish a professional engagement. Claim handling and expert disclosure rules are matters for counsel and the insurer.

MEP Defects Are Not Structural Defects

Structural defect work looks at foundations, framing, connections, and load paths. An MEP construction defect file looks at building systems: equipment selection and installation, distribution (ducts, conductors, piping), controls, and whether the system does what the construction documents or the contract required.

Water from a plumbing failure can damage structure, and a poorly supported rooftop unit can become a structural question. Those overlaps should be scoped separately. A plumbing or HVAC opinion should not be stretched into a structural standard-of-care opinion, and the reverse is just as unhelpful. If the pleading names both, say so at the start of the engagement so the right discipline reviews each allegation.

Installation Defects

Installation defects are departures from the construction documents, manufacturer instructions, or applicable code that show up in how the work was built — not only in how it feels to the occupant. Examples that commonly reach counsel and insurers:

HVAC

Incorrect refrigerant charge practices left undocumented, disconnected or crushed ducts, missing condensate traps, wrong unit orientation, combustion venting that does not match the appliance, or outdoor equipment set with inadequate clearance. Comfort complaints alone are not an installation defect until someone ties them to a specific condition.

Electrical

Undersized conductors for the breaker, missing grounding or bonding, panels installed without working clearance, improper splices, or service equipment that does not match the approved one-line. A later arc or shock investigation is a different assignment; see electrical fire and shock investigations.

Plumbing

Reversed hot and cold, missing vents, improper slope, failed joints, unsupported piping, or fixtures installed without the trap and drainage the drawings showed. Leak-path documentation for water damage is covered in plumbing leak and water-damage investigations.

Performance Failures

A system can be "installed" and still fail to perform. Performance claims ask whether the as-built system can meet the heating, cooling, ventilation, electrical capacity, or drainage result the project required. Typical patterns:

  • HVAC that cannot hold setpoint because equipment, ducts, or ventilation do not match the loads the design assumed
  • Electrical circuits that trip under ordinary use, or a service that cannot carry the equipment the building was sold with
  • Plumbing that drains slowly, surcharges, or leaks again after a localized repair because the layout was never corrected
  • Controls, balancing, or startup that were never completed, so the equipment runs but the system does not

Performance opinions depend on what was specified. Without drawings, submittals, or a written scope, the engineer can still describe observed conditions and code issues, and should state that the original design intent was not available. Carbon monoxide and combustion-venting failures are a subset of HVAC performance and safety questions; see carbon monoxide and HVAC failure investigations.

Standard-of-Care Opinions

Counsel often needs more than a list of defects. A standard-of-care opinion addresses whether the design professional, contractor, or both departed from what a reasonably careful practitioner in that discipline would have done under similar circumstances in Missouri at the time of the work. That opinion is engineering judgment applied to documents and site conditions. It is not a coverage determination and it is not a verdict.

Useful opinions usually separate:

  • Design — sizing, layout, and specifications on the sealed drawings, if any
  • Installation — whether the built work matches those documents and manufacturer limits
  • Maintenance and alteration — later changes, lack of service, or owner work that explains the present condition
  • Code edition — the code and standard in effect for the permit, not today's code applied backward without saying so

How engineering opinions are used in disputes, including the difference between an affidavit and a full report, is discussed in affidavits versus formal expert reports and the PE as expert witness.

When a PE Expert Helps Counsel or an Insurer

A contractor estimate answers what a repair might cost. A PE engagement answers why the condition exists and whether it is consistent with a construction defect rather than wear, weather, or later misuse. Counsel and claim handlers typically bring a PE in when:

  • The pleading or demand names defective HVAC, electrical, or plumbing work on a residence, tenant buildout, or light commercial project
  • Subrogation is being evaluated and the recovery theory depends on how the system was installed
  • Opposing experts have already offered a standard-of-care opinion and a disciplined response is needed
  • Repair scope is disputed — localized fix versus system replacement — and the file needs a technical basis for that scope
  • Multiple trades touched the same leak, odor, or outage and the mechanism is not obvious from photos

Subrogation reporting is outlined in subrogation engineering investigations. Report habits that weaken expert work are covered in common mistakes in engineering expert reports.

What the Work Product Can Cover

Depending on scope, a PE review for an MEP defect claim may include a site visit, document review, and a written opinion that:

  • Describes the system as observed and as shown on drawings
  • Identifies installation or performance conditions tied to specific locations
  • States assumptions, what was not opened or tested, and which documents were missing
  • Compares the work to the cited code, standard, or manufacturer instruction, with the edition identified
  • Offers a standard-of-care opinion only on the disciplines and questions actually scoped
  • Outlines a repair approach at a conceptual level so counsel can compare contractor pricing — not a bid

The opinion should stay inside HVAC, electrical, or plumbing unless a structural or other discipline is added. Services for this kind of review are described under forensic engineering and expert witness support.

Materials That Make the Opinion Usable

  • Complaint, demand, or claim narrative and the questions to be answered
  • Permit set, as-builts, submittals, and approved substitutions
  • Contracts and specifications defining the MEP scope
  • Inspection records, punch lists, and commissioning or startup reports
  • Photos, prior expert reports, and repair invoices
  • Maintenance logs showing what changed after occupancy
  • For HVAC: model numbers, duct sketches, and any load calculations
  • For electrical: panel schedules, one-lines, and service size
  • For plumbing: fixture layouts, pipe material, and leak location
