Tenant Improvement (TI) MEP Permits in Missouri: Electrical, HVAC, and Plumbing Triggers

September 30, 2026

A tenant improvement (TI) is the work that makes a leased space usable for the next occupant: new walls, lighting, HVAC zones, restrooms, exam rooms, or a sales floor. In Missouri, landlords and contractors often treat that work as "just a buildout." Cities and counties still treat changes to electrical, mechanical, and plumbing systems as permitted construction, even when the occupancy group does not change.

Office, retail, and medical TIs are the projects that most often stall in plan review. The architectural layout may be simple. The MEP scope — a new panel, a rooftop unit, added fixtures, or medical equipment — is what the reviewer asks to see on drawings. A licensed Professional Engineer (PE) is more likely to be involved when those systems need sealed calculations or coordinated plans, not because every paint-and-carpet TI needs engineering.

This article is about MEP triggers inside a tenant buildout. A change of occupancy — warehouse to restaurant, retail to assembly — is a separate path and is covered in its own note. Requirements vary by city and county. Confirm them with the authority having jurisdiction before ordering equipment.

TI vs. a Change of Occupancy

Many TIs stay inside the same occupancy. An office suite remains business occupancy. A shop remains mercantile. A clinic remains a business or ambulatory-care use, depending on the services. Plan review can still be required because the systems changed.

A change of occupancy asks whether the building classification itself is different and whether life safety, egress, and structural capacity still fit. A TI permit asks whether the electrical, HVAC, and plumbing work inside the suite is documented and inspectable. Both can apply to the same lease. Treating every buildout as a change of use, or assuming "same occupancy" means no MEP drawings, are both common ways to lose time in review.

Office, Retail, and Medical Buildouts

Office

Typical scope is demising walls, open-office lighting, a conference room, a small server or IDF closet, and restroom adjustments. Reviewers look at lighting and power, whether the existing air handler can serve the new layout, and whether added fixtures fit the water and waste capacity already in the building.

Retail

Sales-floor lighting, a stock room, point-of-sale power, and sometimes a small prep area are common. Food retail can add a mop sink, a water heater, or a grease interceptor even when a full commercial kitchen is not proposed. Those plumbing items are often what turns a "fixture only" permit into a plumbing plan review.

Medical and dental

Exam rooms, procedure lights, imaging or dental equipment, dedicated circuits, and added sinks are usual. Some jurisdictions also ask how ventilation and exhaust are zoned for the suite. Medical TIs are more likely than a standard office to need coordinated electrical and mechanical drawings because equipment loads and exhaust are not obvious from an architectural plan.

Electrical Triggers

An electrical permit is common for almost any TI that adds or relocates power and lighting. Plan review, and sometimes a PE, is more likely when the work includes:

  • A new or replaced panel, feeder, or service connection for the suite
  • Load that exceeds spare capacity in the existing panel
  • Dedicated circuits for medical, kitchen, or IT equipment
  • Lighting alterations large enough that the adopted energy code asks for documentation
  • One-line diagrams the reviewer will not accept as a contractor sketch

A like-for-like device swap in an existing office may stay with a licensed electrician. A suite that adds equipment, a new panel, or a feeder from the building switchboard usually needs a calculation and a drawing the AHJ can review.

HVAC Triggers

Mechanical review is often triggered when the TI changes how air is supplied or exhausted, not merely when a thermostat is moved. Common triggers include:

  • New or relocated rooftop units, split systems, or VAV boxes
  • Duct revisions that change airflow to the new room layout
  • Exhaust for restrooms, copy rooms, dental or medical spaces, or a small prep area
  • Outside-air or ventilation quantities the reviewer wants tied to the new occupant load
  • Equipment schedules and load notes when the existing base building system cannot serve the suite as drawn

Reusing the landlord's air handler is fine when capacity and zoning still work. Reviewers reject sets that show new walls and no corresponding supply, return, or exhaust. Sealed mechanical plans are more likely on commercial and medical suites than on a minor residential-style equipment replacement.

Plumbing Triggers

Plumbing review follows fixtures and water use, not the lease exhibit. Typical TI triggers include:

  • New or relocated restrooms and the fixture count they add
  • Exam sinks, break-room sinks, mop sinks, and water heaters
  • A water-service or meter question when demand increases
  • Grease interceptors or floor sinks when retail includes food prep
  • Backflow protection for equipment the water utility treats as a cross-connection

A single added lavatory in an existing restroom may be a plumbing permit with a simple isometric. A new restroom core, a medical suite, or food retail is more likely to need fixture-unit counts and a plumbing plan. Kitchen hood and grease-duct work, when present, is a mechanical and fire-protection item in addition to the interceptor.

When a Professional Engineer May Be Involved

Contractors and the base-building engineer often carry a straightforward TI. PE documentation for the suite is more likely when:

  • The AHJ requires sealed electrical, mechanical, or plumbing drawings above a stated size or occupancy
  • Office, retail, or medical equipment loads are not covered by the landlord's original design
  • HVAC, exhaust, and plumbing must be coordinated with a new room layout
  • Plan-review comments ask for load calculations, fixture counts, or one-lines the first submittal omitted
  • The tenant's work shares a panel, shaft, or rooftop unit with other suites and the reviewer wants a documented basis

Engineering services may include review of the landlord set, suite-level load and fixture calculations, and drawings or a letter for the permit. Documents are sealed when the scope and available information support responsible charge.

Practical Guidance for Owners and Contractors

  • Ask the city whether the TI is an alteration in the same occupancy or also a change of use
  • Separate electrical, mechanical, and plumbing scopes on the permit application instead of burying them in an architectural set
  • Obtain the base-building panel, HVAC, and riser information before promising the tenant a schedule
  • Show new rooms with the supply, exhaust, power, and fixtures that serve them
  • For medical and food-retail suites, expect equipment loads, exhaust, and fixture counts to be review items
  • Engage a PE when the AHJ asks for sealed MEP drawings or the landlord package does not cover the tenant loads

Closing

Tenant improvement permits in Missouri are often decided by the electrical, HVAC, and plumbing work inside the suite, not only by whether the occupancy group changed. Office, retail, and medical buildouts trip plan review when panels, air distribution, exhaust, or fixtures change. A Professional Engineer may help when the jurisdiction wants sealed MEP documentation or when the existing building systems do not obviously support the tenant's layout.
