Rooftop Solar Structural Review in Missouri: Roof Capacity, Uplift, and Attachment

November 9, 2026

A rooftop solar permit has two different questions. One is electrical: interconnection, rapid shutdown, and batteries. The other is whether the existing roof can carry the array, hold it down in the wind, and accept the attachments without a new leak path or a cracked rafter. Installers searching for a solar panel roof structural engineer in Missouri are usually on the second question.

The solar PV and battery storage article is the electrical path. This article is the structural review: roof capacity, wind uplift, and attachment to the framing that is already there.

This article provides general information. It does not constitute engineering advice or establish a professional engagement. A layout that was accepted on another roof, or in another state, is not a design for this one.

What the Structural Review Is Checking

Modules, rails, and mounts add weight. Wind tries to peel that weight off the roof. The review follows both effects into the existing structure.

  • Dead load of the modules, rails, and mounts, and where that load lands on rafters, trusses, or the roof deck
  • Wind uplift, which is higher at eaves, rakes, and ridges than in the middle of the roof
  • Whether the attachment hardware is being used inside the limits of its listing: roof covering, deck, slope, wind, and snow
  • Whether the existing framing can take those loads without a new sag, a split rafter, or a truss that was never meant to carry them

A walk on the roof that "looks fine" is not that check. Neither is the electrical one-line. Many small residential arrays proceed when the racking letter matches the site. The structural review is what a city asks for when it does not.

Roof Capacity

The roof was built for its own dead load, snow, and the people who reroof it. An array is more dead load, concentrated at the attachments rather than spread like a second layer of shingles. Capacity depends on what is in the attic, not on the module wattage.

  • Rafters. Size, spacing, span, and whether the lags actually hit them. Rails that run the wrong direction can put every attachment between rafters and into the deck only.
  • Trusses. A prefabricated truss is a specific design. Added load on the top chord can be acceptable. Cutting, notching, or hanging the array from the bottom chord is not something the original truss sheet covers. If the truss package did not include the array, the array is a change to that design.
  • Deck. Plywood or oriented strand board that is sound is a different substrate from skip sheathing, spaced boards under an old wood roof, or a deck that is soft at the eaves. Many mount listings do not allow attachment into spaced boards.
  • Condition. A sagging bay, a repaired rafter, two layers of shingles, or a roof already due for replacement changes the conclusion. The array should not be the thing that finds a rotten deck.

Ground snow load in Missouri is a location input, not one number for the state. A capacity letter that used a snow value from another county, or omitted snow because the modules "cover the roof," is not the load for this house. Small residential framing checks of this kind are the work described in small residential structural reviews.

Uplift

Weight holds the array down only until the wind exceeds it. On a pitched residential roof the mounts and the fasteners are what resist uplift. On a low-slope commercial roof a ballasted system may resist uplift by weight and friction, inside the limits of that product's listing, or the array may be attached through the membrane.

Design wind speed is also local. Exposure matters: an open field is not a street of two-story houses. Roof height and roof zones matter more than a single statewide miles-per-hour figure.

  • Edges, corners, and the ridge see higher uplift than the field of the roof. A layout that pushes modules into those zones to clear a chimney or a vent needs the attachment spacing that goes with the zone, not the spacing from the middle of the roof.
  • Fire-access pathways and ridge setbacks, which are often on the electrical or fire sheet, move modules. That move can put more of the array in a higher-uplift zone. The structural layout and the setback layout have to be the same drawing.
  • A ballasted array adds more dead load than a penetrated mount, and it can slide as well as lift. The roof structure still has to carry the ballast. The membrane and the warranty are a separate constraint from the joist check.

A racking table printed for a coastal wind speed, or for a one-story roof, does not automatically cover a two-story Missouri house in open country. The table applies when the site is inside every limit printed on it.

Attachment

Attachment is how the uplift and the dead load get into the framing. The hardware is not interchangeable.

  • Composition shingles. Flashed standoffs, lagged into rafters, are the usual residential path. The flashing is part of the mount. A lag through a shingle with no flashing is a leak, and a leak under an array is expensive to find later.
  • Metal. Standing-seam clamps avoid a penetration when the clamp and the seam are a listed pair. Through-fastened metal is a different mount. A clamp layout from a different seam profile is not that listing.
  • Tile, shake, or skip sheathing. These roofs often need a mount the listing actually names, or they need a different approach than a shingle standoff. Forcing a shingle detail onto spaced boards is a common rejection.
  • Low-slope membrane. Attached mounts penetrate the membrane and have to be flashed in a way the roofing system accepts. Ballast does not penetrate, and it does not fit every building.

The evaluation report for the mount states the fastener, the embedment into the rafter, the minimum rafter size, the deck, and the spacing. Using the report outside those lines is not "the same hardware." How those reports are read in a Missouri submittal is covered in ICC-ES reports. Rail span between attachments follows the same report and the wind zone. A longer span borrowed from a calmer site is a different design.

Fasteners also have to land in a way that does not split the rafter: enough edge distance, not a cluster of lags in one spot, and not a miss that is "close enough" into the deck. The attic photo that shows rafter direction is what makes that layout drawable before anyone is on the roof with a drill.

When a Missouri Review Asks for a Structural PE

Cities and counties adopt and enforce codes locally. Some accept the racking manufacturer's structural letter when the array, the wind, the snow, the roof slope, and the framing match the letter. A site-specific review is the usual request when they do not:

  • The comment already says sealed structural calculations, or the manufacturer letter's wind or snow is below the site
  • The roof is trusses and the array, a crib, or a set of added members is outside the truss design
  • Framing size and span are not on the drawings, which is normal for an existing house with no attic plan
  • The roof is older, already deflecting, or covered with a system the mount listing does not name
  • The array is a ballasted or attached system on a commercial roof, where the joists or bar joists have to be identified

An electrical approval does not answer this list. Interconnection can move forward while the building department is still waiting on the roof letter, or the reverse. They are separate submittals even when they are filed together.

What the Work Product Usually Includes

For an existing residential roof, the package is usually short.

  • The roof framing that was observed or assumed, and what was not seen
  • Dead load of the array and the wind and snow values used
  • A check of the rafters or truss chords those loads reach, or a statement that the manufacturer letter governs because the site is inside it
  • Attachment type, fastener, and spacing, including a tighter spacing at the roof zones that need it
  • A sketch of the array on the roof with the framing direction, ridges, and eaves

Documents are sealed when the scope and the available information support responsible charge. Many of these start as a desktop review from attic photos, a roof plan, and the mount report. A site visit is appropriate when the attic is inaccessible, the deck is suspect, or the jurisdiction requires one. Framing scope for an existing house sits with residential structural review.

What to Send

The electrical set and the structural set share a roof plan. They are not the same pages.

  • Address and the city or county reviewing the building permit
  • Module, rail, and mount cut sheets, and the evaluation report number for the mount
  • Array layout on the roof, with ridges, eaves, chimneys, vents, and the fire setbacks the electrical sheet is using
  • Attic photos showing rafter or truss size, spacing, span, and the deck, plus any sag or prior repair
  • Roof covering, approximate age, slope, and whether the house is one story or two
  • Whether the roof is rafters or trusses, if that is known
  • Any plan-review comment already in hand

A satellite image of the roof is not a substitute for the attic. The direction of the framing is the difference between a lag in a rafter and a lag in the deck.

What This Review Does Not Replace

A rooftop structural review does not:

  • Size the inverter, the rapid shutdown, or the utility interconnection. That scope stays with the electrical article
  • Guarantee that a jurisdiction will accept a manufacturer letter in place of a site-specific calculation
  • Design a reroof, a new roof structure, or a ground-mount foundation. A ground-mounted array is a different structural problem from a roof attachment
  • Approve a mount the evaluation report does not cover for this deck and this wind
  • Confirm production, shading, or that the existing roof has enough years left to justify the array. Those are owner and installer questions. A soft deck is still a reason to stop

Closing

Rooftop solar structural review in Missouri is roof capacity, wind uplift, and attachment. The electrical permit can be complete and the roof question still open. The existing rafters or trusses, the zone of the roof where the modules sit, and the listing for the mount are the inputs. A layout from another house does not carry them.

Photograph the attic before the rails are on the roof. That is when the framing direction, the spans, and a soft deck are still easy to see.
