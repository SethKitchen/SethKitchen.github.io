Removing a Load-Bearing Wall in Missouri: When You Need a Professional Engineer

October 19, 2026

Opening a kitchen, widening a doorway, or taking out the wall between a living room and a dining room is one of the most common residential remodel questions in Missouri. The wall may be only a partition. It may also be holding up floor joists, a roof, or a wall on the story above. Those two cases leave the house in very different condition once the studs are gone.

A licensed Professional Engineer (PE) is the person who calculates the header or beam that replaces a bearing wall, checks the posts and connections, and prepares the documentation a building department can review with the permit. Knocking on drywall, or hearing that "the contractor has done this a hundred times," is not that calculation.

This article provides general information about load-bearing wall removal, header and beam documentation, and when Missouri plan review asks for a PE. It does not constitute engineering advice or establish a professional engagement. Whether a particular wall can be removed, and what member replaces it, depends on the house.

What a Load-Bearing Wall Is Doing

A load-bearing wall carries gravity load from something above it, in addition to its own weight and finishes. That something is often one or more of the following:

  • Floor joists that end on or bear across the wall
  • Ceiling joists or rafters
  • A beam or girder that lands on the wall
  • A bearing wall on the story above, stacked on this one
  • Roof trusses, including a girder truss that collects load from neighboring trusses

A partition divides rooms. It supports its own weight and the finishes attached to it. Removing a true partition still needs a careful look at wiring, ductwork, and plumbing in the cavity, but it does not need a beam to pick up the floor or the roof.

The difference is the load path. While the wall is standing, load from above is spread along the length of the wall and carried down through the studs. When the wall is removed, that load has to be collected by a header or beam and delivered at the posts. The framing those posts land on has to be able to carry a concentrated reaction that used to be distributed.

Openings Homeowners Are Usually Trying to Make

The same engineering question shows up under several project names:

  • Removing the wall between the kitchen and the living or dining room for an open plan
  • A pass-through or a wider cased opening, where part of the wall stays
  • Widening a hallway door, or replacing a swinging door with a wider opening
  • A new or wider exterior opening, such as a patio door or a larger window in a bearing wall
  • Taking out a wall under a second-floor bathroom, a loft, or a bearing wall upstairs

A short pass-through and a full-width opening are not the same span, and a first-floor wall under a second story is not the same load as a wall under an attic. Both can be straightforward once the framing is known. Neither should be sized by copying a beam from a different house.

New additions, which extend the footprint of the house, are a related but separate problem. They are discussed in planning a home addition. This article is about an opening in a wall that is already there.

Clues That Are Not a Determination

People use a handful of field clues to decide whether to call an engineer. The clues are useful for planning. They are not a substitute for looking at the framing.

  • Joists or rafters that run perpendicular to the wall and bear on it are a common sign the wall is carrying those members.
  • Joists that run parallel to the wall often mean that wall is not holding those particular joists. The wall can still carry a load from the story above, a beam, or a girder truss.
  • Exterior walls are commonly bearing. An interior wall can be bearing too, especially near the middle of a wide house.
  • A wall that stacks over a beam or a wall below, or under a wall above, deserves a closer look.
  • A double top plate, a row of studs, or "it feels solid" does not settle the question. Partitions are built that way as well.

Finished ceilings hide the evidence. In a one-story house the attic often shows whether trusses or rafters bear on the wall. In a two-story house the floor framing above the wall matters as much as the roof. Photos of that framing, taken before demolition, are what make a desktop review possible.

Trusses, Joists, and Stacked Walls

Roof construction changes the question.

Prefabricated trusses often span from exterior wall to exterior wall. Many interior walls under that roof are partitions, placed after the trusses were set. That pattern has exceptions a remodel has to catch:

  • A girder truss or a field-framed header in the roof that lands on the interior wall
  • A hip, a valley, or a change in roof geometry that concentrates load
  • An interior wall that supports ceiling joists or a partial second floor even though the roof trusses clear-span
  • Trusses that were never supposed to bear on a partition, but have been shimmed tight to it so the wall is now picking up load

Conventional rafters and ceiling joists more often bear on an interior wall at mid-span. Removing that wall without a beam lets the rafters and ceiling move.

On a two-story house, a first-floor wall under a second-floor bearing wall is carrying the floor, and possibly the roof, from above. The beam has to be sized for that stacked load, and the posts have to line up with support below or with framing that has been checked for the new point load. Floor joist condition, notching, and how joists sit on the wall are part of that picture; see floor joist installation and workmanship.

What Replaces the Wall

If the wall is bearing, something has to take its place before the studs come out. The usual pieces are a header or beam, posts at the ends (and sometimes in between), and the hardware that connects them to the framing that stays.

Header or beam

In an existing wall, a header sits in the wall plane over the opening, with trimmer studs under each end. When the whole wall is removed, the member is a beam. It may be dropped below the ceiling or set flush so the ceiling stays flat and the joists hang from the side of the beam.

A flush beam is what most open-plan remodels want. It is also the arrangement that needs room in the floor or ceiling depth. A dropped beam is often simpler to install and may be shallower in the ceiling space, at the cost of a beam you can see.

Material follows the span and the loads. Built-up sawn lumber, laminated veneer lumber (LVL), parallel strand lumber, glulam, and structural steel are all ordinary choices. Which one fits is a calculation, not a catalog default. A steel beam can be shallower than a wood beam of the same span. It also brings connections, bearing, and sometimes fire-protection questions the drawings have to show.

Posts and the framing they land on

Each end of the beam delivers a concentrated load. Those posts need a path through the existing structure. If a post lands on a joist at mid-span, that joist may need to be doubled, sistered, or picked up by a beam below. If it lands over an existing beam or a stud wall that lines up, the check is shorter. The calculation follows the load until it reaches framing that can carry it.

Homeowners often want a clear span with no post in the middle of the new opening. That is frequently possible. It usually means a deeper member, a flush beam with the right hangers, or both. The tradeoff belongs in the design, before the drywall is stripped.

Strength and stiffness

A beam can be strong enough not to break and still be too flexible. Excessive deflection shows up as a sagging ceiling, a crack at the corner of a remaining wall, or a door on the floor above that no longer latches. Engineering checks both strength and serviceability. A ceiling that has not fallen in the months since an unengineered opening was cut is not either check.

Gravity Load Is Not the Only Question

Some walls also participate in the lateral system that resists wind. Exterior walls, and certain interior walls that were built as braced-wall lines, are the usual cases. An ordinary interior partition often is not part of that system. The way to tell is to look at the bracing, the wall's location, and how the floors and roof are tied together, not to assume every stud wall is a shear wall or that none of them are.

If the wall being removed is a braced-wall line, the design has to show where that bracing goes. That can be a remaining segment of wall, a portal frame, or another method the adopted code allows. It is a separate check from the beam size, and it is one of the reasons a "just put in an LVL" note is not a complete submittal.

When a Missouri Permit Asks for a PE

Cities and counties in Missouri adopt and amend building codes locally. Many use a version of the International Residential Code for one- and two-family houses. That code includes prescriptive header tables. The tables apply only inside the limits printed on them: building width, span, number of floors supported, roof live or snow load, and the header material the table covers.

Plan review commonly asks for engineered calculations when any of the following is true:

  • The opening, the loads, or the member fall outside the prescriptive tables
  • The beam is engineered lumber or steel, and the drawings do not show size, grade, bearing, and connections
  • The wall supports a floor above, a long roof span, or a concentrated load from a girder
  • No framing plans exist, so the reviewer cannot see the load path
  • The comment letter already says "provide sealed structural calculations" or rejects the header as undersized

A permit is typically required to remove or alter a bearing wall even when a prescriptive header would be accepted. Confirm the submittal with the jurisdiction that will review it. Local permit context for several Missouri cities is covered in the Kansas City, St. Louis County, and city permit notes. If a review comment is already in hand, resubmittals after a rejected plan review describes how that comment gets answered.

Roof snow load varies across Missouri. A value taken from a different county, or from a beam that "worked" on another job, is not the load for this house. The adopted code and the local ground snow load are inputs to the calculation, not details to fill in after the member has been ordered.

What the Engineering Work Product Usually Includes

For a residential bearing-wall opening, the work product is usually compact. It is still a set of documents a reviewer and a framer can use:

  • A summary of the scope, the assumptions, and what was and was not observed
  • Gravity loads and, where it matters, a note on lateral bracing
  • Beam or header size, material, and the deflection criteria used
  • Post locations, bearing length, and how joists or rafters connect to the new member
  • A sketch or a marked-up plan that shows the opening and the support path

Documents are sealed when the scope and the available information provide a sufficient basis for responsible charge. Many of these reviews start as a desktop structural review from photos and measurements. A site visit is appropriate when finishes hide the framing, the jurisdiction requires one, or the load path cannot be established from what was submitted. Scope and typical small residential reviews are described under small residential and light commercial structural reviews and on the residential structural service page.

What to Send Before the Wall Is Opened

The useful package is specific. A single photo of the kitchen is not enough to size a beam.

  • Address and the city or county that issues the permit
  • A simple sketch of the floor, with the wall to be removed marked and the desired opening dimensioned
  • Photos of both faces of the wall, and of the rooms on either side, wide enough to see what the wall lines up with
  • Attic or roof-framing photos showing whether members bear on the wall and which way they run
  • Photos of the floor framing above a first-floor wall, from a closet, a ceiling access, or an unfinished area
  • Photos from the level below, if there is one, showing joist direction and any beam the wall sits over
  • Member sizes and spacing where they are visible, the approximate span of the joists or trusses, and ceiling height
  • Any existing drawings, a prior remodel permit, and plan-review comments if the city has already responded

Note what is inside the wall if you know it: a stack vent, a drain, a supply duct, or a cable run. Those are not beam-design inputs, and they are the reason a wall that is structurally simple is still a coordination problem for the contractor.

Temporary Support Is Not the Beam Design

The permanent beam is the engineering document. Temporary shoring that holds the house up while the wall is cut out is construction means and methods. A competent contractor builds that shoring so the load is carried before studs are removed, and leaves it in place until the new beam, posts, and connections are complete.

A sealed beam calculation does not replace that shoring plan. Opening the wall first, to "see what happens," is how ceilings crack and how a remodel becomes an emergency repair. Define the permanent member, then shore, then demolish.

If the Wall Is Already Gone

Some requests start after the studs are out. A beam may already be in place, or the ceiling may be spanning temporarily on framing that was never checked. An engineer can often still evaluate what is there and prepare documentation for a permit or a correction, with two limits.

  • The original load path is harder to prove once the wall and the finishes are gone. Assumptions have to be stated, and a site visit is more likely.
  • Distress that is already visible — a new sag, cracks radiating from the opening, or doors binding on the floor above — is part of the condition being evaluated. The repair may be more than "add a bigger beam" if the supported framing has already moved.

Work performed without a permit is a related problem, covered in engineering documentation for unpermitted work. Legalizing an opening still depends on the city's process and on whether the installed member can be shown to work.

What This Engineering Does Not Replace

A structural review for a bearing-wall opening does not:

  • Guarantee that a jurisdiction will approve the permit
  • Design the architectural layout, the kitchen, or the finishes
  • Relocate plumbing, HVAC, or electrical that is inside the wall
  • Provide the contractor's temporary shoring plan
  • Size a beam from a verbal span with no information about what the wall supports

It does give the header or beam, the posts, and the load path a reviewer can check, and a framer can build, when the existing structure has been adequately described.

Closing

Removing a load-bearing wall in Missouri is a permit and a calculation problem whenever the wall is carrying a floor, a roof, or a wall above. Prescriptive header tables cover some simple openings. Longer spans, stacked floors, engineered lumber, steel, and missing framing plans are the situations where a Professional Engineer prepares the header and beam documentation.

The reliable sequence is to identify what the wall supports, size the permanent member and its posts, submit that with the permit, and only then shore and demolish. Photos of the framing before the wall is opened are what make that sequence possible.
