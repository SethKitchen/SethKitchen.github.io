Structural Construction Defect Claims in Missouri: Framing, Connections, and Standard of Care

November 18, 2026

Counsel and insurers in Missouri see construction defect files that are about the frame. A header is smaller than the drawings, joist hangers are missing, a truss was cut to make room for a duct, or a deck ledger is fastened in a way the listing does not allow. The house may be standing. The question is whether the framing and the connections met the standard of care that applied when the work was done, and what repair follows from that condition.

A licensed Professional Engineer (PE) can help when the file needs a technical opinion a repair estimate cannot carry: the load path, the departure from the drawings or from accepted framing practice, and a repair scope counsel can compare to a bid. The PE does not decide coverage, assign legal fault, or replace counsel's theory of the case.

This article is the framing-and-connection companion to the HVAC, electrical, and plumbing construction defect article. It addresses headers, joists, roof framing, decks, and what a standard-of-care opinion covers. It does not address foundation claims. It does not constitute legal or engineering advice or establish a professional engagement. Claim handling and expert disclosure rules are matters for counsel and the insurer.

Framing Defects Are Not MEP Defects

An MEP file looks at equipment, ducts, conductors, and piping. A framing file looks at members and the hardware that ties them together: headers and beams, floor joists, roof rafters and trusses, and deck ledgers and hangers. Both can appear in the same pleading. They are not the same opinion.

A duct chased through a joist, or a rooftop unit set on framing that was not checked, can be both. Scope them separately. A framing opinion should not be stretched into an HVAC standard-of-care opinion, and a plumbing opinion should not be asked to size a header. If the demand names both, say so when the engagement starts.

Headers, Joists, Roof Framing, and Decks

The allegations that show up in these files are specific. A general statement that the house was "not built to code" is not yet an engineering question.

Headers and beams

Openings for garage doors, removed walls, and widened windows depend on a header or beam, posts, and bearing. The dispute is often that the member in the wall is smaller than the drawings, is the wrong material, bears on a stud that was not a post, or delivers its reaction onto a joist at mid-span. Removing a wall and leaving no beam at all is the same family of question, discussed from the permit side in removing a load-bearing wall. In a claim, the issue is what was built, not what a future remodel might do.

Floor joists

Joist claims turn on size, spacing, span, bearing, hangers, and holes or notches that remove wood where the joist needs it. Sistering that does not extend to a support, or hangers with fasteners in only some of the holes, shows up the same way: the floor is bouncy or the ceiling below has cracked, and the photos do not match the joist layout on the plans. Signs of poor joist work are described in floor joist installation and workmanship.

Roof framing

Rafter roofs and truss roofs fail for different reasons. A rafter claim may be undersized members, a ridge connection, or missing ties. A truss claim is often a cut or drilled chord, missing bracing required by the truss drawings, or trusses set at a spacing the layout did not use. The truss placement drawings and the bracing notes are part of the construction documents. A roof that has not leaked is not evidence that those notes were followed.

Deck framing and connections

Deck claims in this scope are the ledger, the joist hangers, the beam-to-post connection, and the lateral connection back to the house. A ledger fastened only into veneer, hangers installed with the wrong fastener, or a guard that was part of the permitted deck and was not built are framing and connection questions. Post embedment and footing claims are a different assignment from this article.

Connections Carry the Load Path

A member can be the right size and still be a defect if it is not connected. The opinion has to look at the hardware, not only the lumber stamp.

  • Hangers, clips, and straps called for on the drawings or by the truss and joist manufacturer, including fasteners in the holes the listing uses
  • Toenails where a hanger was specified, or screws substituted for nails when the hanger is not listed for that screw
  • Bearing length at a beam, a wall, or a hanger seat that is too short for the reaction
  • Bolted connections with missing bolts, oversized holes, or a bolt pattern that does not match the detail
  • A load that was supposed to continue down through a post and instead stops on a plate or a joist that was never checked

Photos of the connection before drywall, or a selective opening where counsel authorizes it, are what make this opinion possible. A view of the finished ceiling does not show the hanger.

What a Standard-of-Care Opinion Covers

Counsel often needs more than a list of crooked studs. A standard-of-care opinion addresses whether the design professional, the framer, or both departed from what a reasonably careful practitioner in that role would have done under similar circumstances in Missouri at the time of the work. That opinion is engineering judgment applied to documents and to what can be observed. It is not a coverage determination and it is not a verdict.

Useful framing opinions usually separate:

  • Design — member sizes, layout, and connection details on the sealed drawings or the truss package, if any. A house built from prescriptive framing tables may never have had a design engineer. The opinion should say so instead of criticizing a seal that does not exist.
  • Installation — whether the built framing matches those documents, the approved hardware, and ordinary framing practice for that connection
  • Later alteration — an owner, a later contractor, or a remodel that cut, notched, or removed a member after the original work. That condition can explain the crack without being the original builder's defect.
  • Code edition — the residential or building code the jurisdiction had adopted for that permit, not today's code applied backward without saying so. Missouri jurisdictions adopt codes locally, and the edition on the permit card matters.

How engineering opinions are used in disputes, including the difference between an affidavit and a full report, is discussed in affidavits versus formal expert reports and the PE as expert witness.

When a PE Expert Helps Counsel or an Insurer

A contractor estimate answers what a repair might cost. A PE engagement answers whether the condition is consistent with a framing or connection defect, and what has to be repaired for the load path to work. Counsel and claim handlers typically bring a PE in when:

  • The pleading or demand names defective framing, a missing header, cut trusses, or a deck connection on a residence or light commercial project
  • Repair scope is disputed — a sistered joist and a hanger, versus replacing a bay of framing — and the file needs a technical basis for that scope. How engineers frame repair versus replacement is covered in repair versus replace after structural damage
  • Subrogation is being evaluated and the recovery theory depends on how the frame was built
  • An opposing expert has already offered a standard-of-care opinion and a response has to stay inside framing and connections
  • The visible crack or sag could also be later alteration, a plumbing chase, or ordinary shrinkage, and the mechanism is not obvious from the demand letter

Subrogation reporting is outlined in subrogation engineering investigations. Report habits that weaken expert work are covered in common mistakes in engineering expert reports.

What the Work Product Can Cover

Depending on scope, a PE review for a framing defect claim may include a site visit, document review, and a written opinion that:

  • Describes the framing as observed and as shown on the permit drawings, truss package, or deck details
  • Identifies members and connections at specific locations, not a house-wide conclusion from one photo
  • States assumptions, what was not opened, and which documents were missing
  • Compares the work to the cited drawing, code edition, or manufacturer instruction
  • Offers a standard-of-care opinion only on the framing and connection questions actually scoped
  • Outlines a repair approach at a conceptual level so counsel can compare contractor pricing — not a bid, and not a percentage allocation of fault

The opinion should stay inside framing and connections unless another discipline is added. Services for this kind of review are described under forensic engineering and expert witness support.

What to Send

The useful file is the construction record plus the dispute, not a stack of interior photos alone.

  • The pleading, demand, or reservation of rights that identifies the alleged defect
  • Permit drawings, sealed structural sheets if any, truss placement drawings, and approved deck details
  • Photos of the members and connections, including attic, crawl space or basement ceiling, and deck ledger, taken before repair if they exist
  • The permit date and the jurisdiction, so the code edition can be identified
  • Change orders, remodel permits, and any later work that cut or removed framing
  • The opposing expert's report, if one has been served

A site visit can still be useful when the framing is concealed. Opening finishes is a decision for counsel and the owner, and the opinion should say what was not opened.

What This Opinion Does Not Replace

A framing standard-of-care review does not:

  • Decide coverage, allocation of fault, or the outcome of the case
  • Substitute for an HVAC, electrical, or plumbing opinion when the pleading names those systems
  • Substitute for a foundation opinion. This article does not cover footing, slab, or settlement claims
  • Produce a contractor's bid, a repair contract, or a sealed set of remedial drawings unless that scope is added
  • Treat every crack, squeak, or out-of-level floor as a construction defect. Some of those conditions are shrinkage, later alteration, or ordinary performance of wood framing

Closing

Structural construction defect claims in Missouri that turn on framing are about members and connections: headers, joists, roof framing, and deck hardware, compared with the drawings and the practice that applied at the time. A standard-of-care opinion separates design, installation, and later alteration, and it names the code edition instead of using today's code as if it had been on the permit.

The MEP article remains the right starting point when the defect is equipment, wiring, or piping. When the defect is the frame, the opinion has to be about the frame.
